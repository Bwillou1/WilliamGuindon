Partie 01Part 01 The story, told simply

In Blainville, in the Laurentians north of Montreal, there is a large swamp called Grande Tourbière (Great Peat Bog). A bog is a spongy wetland where dead vegetation accumulates without completely rotting. These environments filter water, store carbon and shelter species that are found nowhere else.

Next door is a company called Stablex. Its job: to receive dangerous industrial waste – sludge filled with heavy metals, for example – to mix it with cement to “freeze” it, then to bury it in large airtight pits called cells. Its first five cells reach saturation. He needs a sixth.

The land coveted for this cell no. 6 belonged to the Ville de Blainville (City of Blainville), which refused to cede it. In March 2025, the Quebec government resolved the problem in an expeditious manner: it passed a special law, Loi 93 (Bill 93), passed overnight under a gag order (an emergency procedure that cuts short the debate). This law expropriates the City, pays it $17 million, transfers the land to Stablex — and in the process forecloses most recourses before the courts. The City and the Metropolitan Community of Montreal tried to challenge it: they lost in the Superior Court on April 2, 2025, then in the Court of Appeal on April 16, 2025.

The machines have arrived. The trees have fallen. It was at this moment that William Guindon, a citizen of Blainville then aged 14 at the start of his efforts (now 15 years old), opened an unprecedented legal front: rather than attacking Quebec or Stablex – two avenues blocked by the Loi 93 – he questioned the direct responsibility of Ottawa.

His legal reasoning is implacable:

Quebec can legislate on the transfer of land, but there are Canadian federal laws that protect fish habitat, migratory birds and species at risk across the country, including on private or municipal land. These laws fall under the exclusive jurisdiction of Ottawa. And Ottawa remained completely silent.

The strength of the case lies in this demonstration: it does not criticize the federal government for having poorly exercised its discretionary power, but for never having exercised it. No known federal inspections, no permits issued or refused under the Fisheries Act or the Species at Risk Act, no reasoned decisions.

By mobilizing the international mechanism of chapter 24 of the ACEUM (SEM process) from May 1, 2026, then by filing on July 16, 2026 a consolidated communication of exceptional density - 15-page brief, 102-page analytical annex and 222 evidentiary documents -, he has built a file that stands the test of international filtering.

On August 17, 2026, the Commission de coopération environnementale (Commission for Environmental Cooperation) made a historic determination: the communication met all of the admissibility criteria of the North American treaty. The Government of Canada is legally required to respond by October 16, 2026.

Partie 03Part 03 The place: three superimposed layers

3.1 — The Grande Tourbière de Blainville

A wetland complex which, according to provincial inspection reports on file, includes “a marsh, a swamp, shallow water and a peat bog”. There we find plants indicative of wetlands — pitcher plant (a carnivorous plant), cotton grass — growing on permanent sphagnum mats. The Bureau d'audiences publiques sur l'environnement (BAPE), in its report No. 371, highlighted the “exceptional ecological value” of the sector and advised the government against authorizing the project.

A geological detail that matters enormously. The area is underlain by thick deposits of clay from the Champlain Sea — a sea that covered the St. Lawrence Valley about 10,000 years ago. These clays, 15 meters thick on the surface and up to 50 meters deep, are subject to “deep ravinement”: the water creates tree-shaped networks. In practical terms, this means that any sediment or contaminant that reaches the ground is transported quickly and far to waterways. It is not a ground that holds back; it is a ground that leads.

3.2 — Cell No. 6

The land in question is lot 6 695 053 of the Quebec land register. According to media coverage and BAPE (Québec Bureau of Public Environmental Hearings), it includes approximately 9 hectares of wetlands and 58 hectares of woodlands. According to the proponent's impact study, the 52-hectare study area contains 29.2 hectares of wetlands (56% of the surface area), including a 19.1-hectare tree swamp. The planned backfilling would cover 278,000 m², or 27.8 hectares. (These different figures relate to different perimeters — see Part 9.)

The agreement in principle signed on March 11, 2020 between the City and the developer provides for 24 hours a day, 7 days a week operation. The new site would allow Stablex to operate for around forty more years, until around 2065.

The physical reality of landfilling raised on the record
While promotional materials refer to solidified “blocks”, the technical expert testimony entered into the record (timestamped transcript) describes a semi-liquid cementitious sludge prone to phase separation: “It is like poorly mixed cement. […] Water floats on top of the cement. […] Those are not blocks; it is sludge being buried.” As for the impermeable geomembrane, the specialist interviewed views it as “false security” over the long term against chemical degradation, ground shifting, or seismic events. These technical elements entered into the registry aim to shed light on long-term hydrogeological risks to the receiving environment.

3.3 — What was there before: Camp Bouchard

This is perhaps the most surprising element of the file. From 1942 to 1980, this same land was home to Camp Bouchard, a munitions production plant and explosives depot operated by the Canadian Department of National Defense. In other words: the soil was first contaminated by the federal government itself.

The site still appears on the Federal Contaminated Sites Inventory (number 06875001), maintained by the Treasury Board Secretariat. According to the data cited in the complaint:

  • Identified contaminants: metals, metalloids and organometallics in groundwater and soil
  • File status: “Active” — it is not closed
  • Management strategy: “Additional assessment” — work is not completed
  • Expenditures in 2025-2026: $0 on evaluation, $0 on restoration, $0 on monitoring
  • Area restored in 2025-2026: 0 hectare
  • Historical total expenditures: approximately $147,000 for a 4-hectare military site operated for 38 years

Stablex itself publicly attributes part of the metals detected to this previous military use. The author draws two formidable questions from this: (1) did the Ministry of Defense decontaminate before ceding the land? A freedom of information request reportedly found no documentation of decontamination work. (2) If the metals found in the stream come in part from the former federal military site, why has Ottawa never investigated?

Partie 04Part 04 The mechanism, from A to Z

4.1 — Where does this tool come from

In 1994, when signing NAFTA, the three countries feared that one state would be tempted to attract factories by turning a blind eye to pollution. They therefore created, in parallel, an environmental agreement equipped with a citizen valve. When NAFTA was replaced by ACEUM on July 1, 2020, this valve was retained and written directly into the trade treaty, in articles 24.27 and 24.28.

Curiosity of vocabulary: the official French text of ACEUM speaks of “observations on questions of application”. The Commission continues to use the word “communications”. Both mean exactly the same thing: a citizen complaint. The acronym SEM comes from the English Submissions on Enforcement Matters.

4.2 — First question: what is an “environmental law”?

All the legal sorting of the decision follows from this definition. Here is the official text, in article 24.1 of ACEUM:

“environmental law means a law or regulation of a Party, or one of its provisions […] the primary object of which is the protection of the environment, or the prevention of a danger to health or human life, by, as the case may be: a) the prevention, reduction or control of the discharge […] of pollutants or contaminants; (b) the control of dangerous or toxic chemicals, substances, materials or waste […]; (c) the protection or conservation of wild flora and fauna, including endangered species, their habitat and specially protected natural areas […]” “law or regulation means […] for Canada, an Act of the Parliament of Canada or a regulation made under an Act of the Parliament of Canada which may be enforced by action of the central government. »
In plain language

For an article of law to be retained by the CCE (Commission for Environmental Cooperation), it must meet three strict conditions: (1) have as its primary purpose the protection of the environment or human health; (2) be a federal law or regulation passed in Ottawa; (3) fall under the application of the federal government. It is this surgical filter that allowed the Secretariat to focus the investigation on the four federal ecological pillars invoked in the communication (Fisheries Act, SARA, LCOM, CEPA), while ruling out provisions of a purely commercial or general constitutional nature.

An individual validation of 100% of admissibility criteria. In its August 17, 2026 determination, the Secretariat underscored the rigor of its review: rather than making a broad assessment, it evaluated each criterion of paragraphs 24.27(1), (2), and (3) individually. A single missing criterion is enough to trigger dismissal — the fate of the vast majority of submissions filed in North America. Relying on its settled jurisprudence (Loggerhead Turtle 2021, Fairview Terminal 2021, Vaquita 2021), the Secretariat concluded that the dossier consolidated by William Guindon successfully passed every one of these demanding filters.

An important counterbalance, that said: the Secretariat also recalls that these criteria “must not constitute an insurmountable procedural obstacle” and must be interpreted in the broad sense. He cites three precedents in this sense (Loggerhead Turtle 2021, Fairview Terminal 2021, Gulf of California Porpoise 2021). The door is narrow, but we must not barricade it.

4.5 — Deadlines, calculated to the nearest day

The Secretariat explains in a footnote how it counts: in calendar days; the day of departure is not counted, the day of arrival is; if the deadline falls on a Saturday, Sunday or public holiday, it slides to the next working day; a document received on a non-working day is deemed received on the following working day.

4.7 — The nature of the SEM mechanism: the power of the international investigation

✔ International powers and leverage
  • Force the federal government to come out of its silence by submitting a written, official and public response on the application of its laws.
  • Present the facts on a North American scale before the Council of Ministers of the Environment of Canada, the United States and Mexico.
  • Commission an independent investigation (the factual record) involving scientific experts, interviews with authorities and unassailable technical verifications.
  • Create an official evidentiary basis that can be used by parliamentarians, researchers, the media and the courts.
Distinction from a domestic court of law
  • The Secretariat is not a civil or criminal court issuing fines or direct construction injunctions against private companies.
  • Its authority is exercised at the higher level of interstate accountability and transparency of environmental public action.

The CCE Secretariat is not a court of first instance, but a higher international institution of transparency and control of law enforcement. The major strength of the SEM process lies in its unique capacity to establish an incontestable public documentary truth, instructed by independent experts, forcing the State to answer for its actions before its trinational partners of the ACEUM and international public opinion.

Partie 05Part 05 The complaint, allegation by allegation

The revised communication is 15 pages long, accompanied by an analytical annex of 102 pages and more than 200 documents. It was signed in Blainville on July 16, 2026 and ends with this sentence: “Progress is not measured by the number of profits we generate, but by the number of contaminants we refuse to leave to our children. »

The rule the submitter imposes on himself
At the outset, he specifies that the words “omission”, “failure”, “discharge”, or “contamination” used in the submission designate allegations of fact and law subject to verification, not findings of liability. He adds that all references to Quebec statutes and decrees are submitted solely as factual background and material evidence to demonstrate that the works, discharges, and contamination exist. His target is “strictly and solely the failure of the Government of CANADA to enforce its own federal environmental laws”. That discipline paid off: the Secretariat explicitly relied on it to set aside provincial instruments in its legal review while accepting them as evidence of the facts.
Allégation 1

Species at risk

Species at Risk Act (SARA), sections 32, 33, 58, 73, and 79

The articles, in plain English:

  • Art. 32 — prohibits killing, injuring, harassing, capturing or taking an individual of a species listed as extinct, endangered or threatened.
  • Art. 33 — prohibits damaging or destroying the residence of these individuals (their nest, their burrow, their hibernacula).
  • Art. 58 — prohibits destruction of critical habitat. Crucial point: this ban applies to aquatic species and migratory birds even outside federal lands. It is the gateway to private land.
  • Sec. 58 (Critical Habitat) — A precise legal demonstration: Rather than invoking section 58 generically, the 102-page analytical annex (section 3.2.3) surgically targeted subsection 58(5.1) relating to migratory birds as well as protection obligations outside federal lands. That legal precision paid off: whereas section 58 had not been retained in the initial screening, the Secretariat formally validated and included it in the review on August 17, 2026.
  • Art. 73 — this is the article of the permit. The Minister may authorize an activity affecting a listed species, but only if all other options have been considered, if all minimization measures are taken, and if the survival of the species is not jeopardized. The decision must be reasoned and entered in the public register.
  • Art. 79 — requires anyone assessing the environmental effects of a project to notify the Minister if a listed species is likely to be affected.

The reproach: deforestation and backfilling work takes place where species protected by federal law live, without any permit, any verification of applicability, or any reasoned decision appearing in public records.

Allégation 2

Migratory birds

Migratory Birds Convention Act, 1994 and Regulations (2022 version). Sometimes abbreviated ROM in the complaint.

The articles, in plain English:

  • Art. 5.1(1) of the Act — prohibits the discharge of a substance harmful to migratory birds into the waters or regions they frequent, or into any other place from which the substance could reach them. This last clarification is decisive: it is not necessary to discharge directly into the pond; the product just needs to be able to get there.
  • Art. 5(1) of the Regulations — prohibits, unless permitted: (a) capturing, killing, injuring or harassing a migratory bird; b) destroy or disturb an egg; c) damage, destroy, remove or disturb a nest.

The heaviest word in this regulation is “disturb”. It is not necessary to kill a bird or destroy a nest: disturbance is enough. It is on this word that the argument for work carried out during the nesting period is based.

What is being said:

  • 132 species of birds recorded during the breeding season in the plots of the Atlas of Breeding Birds of Quebec corresponding to the sector; according to the 2025 provincial report, 66% were breeding and 34% probable or possible breeding.
  • During specific surveys in June 2016: 172 individuals of 51 species of protected migratory birds counted in the planned cutting area — including the Red-winged Blackbird, the Common Yellowthroat, the Fox Thrush, the Oven-crowned Warbler, the White-throated Sparrow.
  • On May 22, 2026, during the critical period of protected nesting, citizens photographed the arrival of a semi-trailer delivering several industrial lighting towers to the construction site. With strict factual rigor, the file distinguishes the material proof of the deployment of nighttime equipment from their continued use, while demonstrating the direct risk of light disturbance within the meaning of Article 5(1) of the Regulation.
  • Mortality of common loons, protected migratory birds, reported by Radio-Canada Enquête on October 9, 2025.
  • Documented releases of cadmium, chromium and suspended solids into a network connected to the stream — precisely the scenario targeted by section 5.1(1).
  • No ECCC permits identified in public records.

An element that the Secretariat itself noted: a document from the Quebec Ministry of the Environment, the 2024 “request for commitments”, requires Stablex to take nesting periods into account, explicitly refers to ECCC tools to avoid harming migratory birds, and asks it to “present measures to be put in place to protect the nests” of the pileated woodpecker, the great blue heron and the green heron before the end of the period waiting period provided for in federal regulations. In other words: Quebec referred to the federal framework. Which makes the absence of any trace of federal intervention all the more striking.

Allégation 3 — l'axe principal

The Fisheries Act

Articles 34.4, 35, 36(3), 36(4), 37 et 38

This is the heart of the case, and by far the most powerful legal weapon. For what ? Because these prohibitions apply regardless of who owns the land. The Loi 93 was able to transfer ownership, it did not change the fact that there are fish downstream.

The articles, in plain English:

  • Art. 34.4 — prohibits the exploitation of a work, business or activity that results in the death of fish (except fishing itself), unless authorized.
  • Art. 35 — prohibits any activity resulting in the deterioration, destruction or disturbance of fish habitat, unless authorized by the Minister. It is the most cited article in Canada on wetlands.
  • Art. 36(3) — prohibits the dumping or discharge of a harmful substance into waters frequented by fish, or in any other place from which it may reach them. Here again: the gap matters.
  • Art. 36(4) — provides for exceptions: discharges authorized by regulation, in specific quantities and conditions. Added to the analysis in the second decision.
  • Art. 37 — allows the Minister to require documents from the operator, then modify, restrict or cease the activity.
  • Art. 38 — organizes inspections. It also creates an obligation to report: everyone must report to the authorities the death of fish, the destruction of their habitat or the unauthorized discharge of a harmful substance, take corrective measures and produce a report.

a) Are there really fish?

This is the first thing a government could challenge. The response to the file is massive. Scientific fishing carried out in August 2016 in the site's ditches — using electricity and fishing rods — captured 155 specimens of 9 species: mudfish (108), redfin shiner (17), fivespine stickleback (14), pumpkinseed, white sucker, pearl mullet, yellow-bellied darter, fathead minnow.

Better: it is the study of the promoter himself which recognizes that “the fish habitat is considered potential over the entire length of the watercourse studied”. She specifies that “all of the streams in the study area are in fact ditches […] and the water in the ditches comes from wetlands present on the outskirts. It flows slowly towards natural streams located outside the study area.” Some of these ditches include mature aquatic grass beds and beaver reservoirs reaching 2 meters deep. A 2020 government inspection report confirms a network of interconnected ditches actively draining eastward.

Logical conclusion of the file: it is not an isolated hole in the middle of a field. It is a connected hydraulic system, which leads to Lockhead Creek, then to Fauvel Lakes. Anything that falls in travels.

Allegation 4 — presented in the alternative

Toxic substances and the right to a healthy environment

Canadian Environmental Protection Act (1999), modernized in 2023 to include the right to a healthy environment.

A major legal novelty, still little known. Since the Right to a Healthy Environment Act, assented to on June 13, 2023, CEPA requires the federal government to “protect the right of every individual in Canada to a healthy environment […] subject to reasonable limits”, respecting the principles of non-regression, intergenerational equity and environmental justice — which includes the prevention of harmful effects disproportionately affecting vulnerable populations.

In plain language

Since 2023, there has been a legal right to a healthy environment in Canada. And this right must be applied with future generations in mind. An adolescent who invokes this right for himself and for those who come after him is therefore not using a figure of speech: he is relying on a brand new legal text, which no one has yet really tested. This is, legally, the most innovative aspect of the case.

What is being said:

  • Between 2019 and 2024, the site would have received 120,837 tonnes of soil from the Orrington site (Maine, United States), linked to the Mallinckrodt company. 84% of this volume — 101,951 tonnes — would be classified as hazardous residue due to its mercury content. Mercury is a very volatile metal.
  • These operations take place 370 to 400 meters from residential and commercial areas.
  • The company's release inventory lists fine particles PM2.5, hexavalent chromium (recognized carcinogen), mercury, arsenic, nickel, crystalline silica and ammonia. An Englobe report would confirm the physical presence of hexavalent chromium and crystalline silica in the dust from the site.
  • The framework for implementing the right to a healthy environment, required by section 5.1(1) of CEPA, was due no later than June 13, 2025.

The criticism: ECCC would not have exercised any of its powers of evaluation (art. 68), collection of information (art. 71) or risk management (art. 90 and 91) with regard to this site.

Articulation with commercial (art. 24.4) and constitutional (art. 35) law. The Loi 93 as a lock. This is the most political argument. When a provincial government uses its parliament to neutralize local counter-powers and close legal recourse, the author maintains, the application of federal laws becomes “the one and only bulwark”. The “discretion/absence of decision” distinction. This is the finest reasoning in the file. Article 24.4(2) of ACEUM explicitly recognizes that a country retains the right to exercise discretion in matters of investigation and allocation of resources; acting reasonably in exercising this discretion is not a violation. The author concedes this from the outset - then turns the argument around: we can only speak of a discretionary choice if a power has first been exercised. Here, there would be no inspection, no refusal, no reasoned decision. So not misused discretion, but a pure and simple absence of exercise. The argument a fortiori on the precedents of the CCE. By retaining the most powerful provisions of Canadian environmental law — the Fisheries Act, the Species at Risk Act, the Migratory Birds Convention Act, and CEPA — the CEC focused the inquiry on the real ecological impacts suffered by habitats and species.
6. The prospect of an independent factual record
If the Secretariat's recommendation is confirmed by the trinational Council, a team of independent experts will be mandated to conduct an in-depth public investigation, question officials, and establish the facts irrefutably.

Le paradoxe du silence
The treaty text says the Party “may” provide a response — it is an option, not an absolute obligation. However, if Canada fails to respond, or responds evasively, the Secretariat evaluates the submission “in light of any response provided”: an empty response refutes nothing. That is precisely the calculation the submitter laid out in advance in his submission. Ottawa therefore faces an uncomfortable choice: document its enforcement action — if any exists — or let the silence speak for itself.

Partie 10Part 10 The Questions Everyone Asks

Can a minor really file an international complaint?
Yes. The treaty speaks of “any person of a Party”: no age requirement. A tasty paradox: he cannot go before a court in his own country without legal representation, but he can refer the matter to a trinational commission. Moreover, it is precisely this internal impossibility which allowed it to satisfy the criterion of private remedies.

Why target Ottawa and not Quebec, when it is Quebec that authorized everything?
Two reasons. First, the legal: the SEM mechanism is only aimed at central governments — Quebec literally cannot be targeted. Then, the strategic: the Loi 93 closed the provincial appeals, and two courts confirmed it in April 2025. Only the federal one remained.

How can a federal law apply to private land in Quebec?
By fish and birds. Fish and their habitat, as well as migratory birds, fall under federal jurisdiction across the country, regardless of who owns the land. Section 36(3) of the Fisheries Act prohibits the discharge of a deleterious substance into waters frequented by fish “or to any other place” from which it may arrive — a ditch, for example. This is what makes this article so powerful.

If the factual record is published, what is its real impact?
Its institutional, scientific and political impact is major. The factual record is an independent international investigation led by the three governments of North America. It allows neutral experts to be commissioned, the law enforcement practices of federal departments to be examined, and the public factual and scientific truth to be set in stone. In several historical precedents (such as the SEM-97-005 or North Atlantic Right Whale affair), these reports have forced administrative reforms, fueled parliamentary work and served as key evidence in the public space.

Has this mechanism already worked?
He produced published factual files, including against Canada. The most famous in this context is SEM-97-005, on acid discharges in the St. Lawrence: the investigators appointed by the Secretariat concluded that Environment Canada had modified an inspection report to the point that the signing official declared that he no longer recognized it. The Secretariat has also just published, in December 2025, a factual file on the North Atlantic right whale (SEM-21-003), and in August 2025 another on the Gulf of California porpoise.

How long can the procedure last given the progress of the work?
Submitted in May 2026, the process is moving through its stages at a sustained pace: following the submission of Canada's response (72-RSP) on October 16, 2026 and the citizen's reply of October 17, 2026, the Secretariat has 60 working days (art. 24.28(1) ACEUM) to recommend a factual file, while federal ministers are legally required to respond to BVG Petition No. 0546 by November 10, 2026. If the construction machinery operates in the short term, the SEM mechanism establishes federal responsibility over time: each destruction of habitat or release of contaminants occurring during Ottawa's inaction increases the factual toll for which the government will have to answer to its ACEUM partners.

What is the value and scope of the file filed by William Guindon?
The legal result is exceptional: while the vast majority of citizen communications are rejected at the preliminary stage of admissibility by the Secretariat of the CCE, that of William Guindon successfully passed all the admissibility criteria of articles 24.27(1), (2) and (3) of the ACEUM. The Secretariat has validated the probative force of its corpus of 222 documentary pieces (university hydrogeological studies, wildlife characterizations, accredited laboratory analyzes of cadmium exceedances, ministerial inspection records). It is a tour de force all the more remarkable because it was accomplished in a completely autonomous and voluntary manner by a 15-year-old citizen, reaching where no municipality or any Quebec legal action had succeeded in forcing the State to be publicly accountable.

Commission de coopération environnementale. Trinational organization created in 1994, based in Montreal. Three bodies: Council, Secretariat, Joint Public Advisory Committee.

Waterproof compartment dug into the ground, isolated by a membrane, where waste is placed. Stablex operates five; the sixth is the subject of the dispute.
CER (rare endpoint)
Reference threshold for sediments: level below which virtually no toxic effects on bottom-dwelling organisms are expected.
CVAC (chronic aquatic life criterion)
Reference threshold for water: concentration beyond which prolonged exposure harms aquatic organisms.
Hexavalent chromium
Particularly toxic form of chromium, recognized carcinogen by inhalation. Not to be confused with trivalent chromium, which is much less dangerous.
Communication / Observation
The two names for the same thing: the citizen complaint. The treaty says “observations”, the Commission says “communications”.

The environment ministers of the three countries, or their representatives. This is the body that votes on the creation of a factual file — by 2 votes out of 3.

Quebec decree authorizing the project and setting its conditions. The provincial notices of non-compliance mentioned in the file relate to compliance with these conditions.
Determination
The written and reasoned decision of the Secretariat at each stage of filtering. This is not a judgment.

The possible end product of the process: a public investigation report bringing together technical, scientific and legal data, interviews with officials and analyzes from independent experts. He states the facts without concluding, for the public to judge.
ECCC

ICP-MS
