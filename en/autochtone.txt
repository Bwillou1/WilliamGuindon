## A broad file, not a legend.

The broadest portrait that open sources allow us to write in 2026: the people, the peat bog, the
surrounding water territories, the species. The thickness is over time. Nothing is invented to fill
a silence.

A civic and rigorous approach to documentary research. Each fact is linked to a verifiable source:
public archives, ministries, BAPE (Québec Bureau of Public Environmental Hearings), journalistic
investigations, ecological studies and direct transmissions. When data diverge, the contradiction is
exposed with transparency and honesty.

It is neither a speaking out on behalf of the First Nations, nor an impersonal synthesis produced on
an assembly line by algorithms. This document does not replace either the living memory of the
elders, nor the sovereign teachings of the Clan Mothers of the Longhouse. Colonial archives have
often obscured the indigenous presence on these lands; this work seeks to name this silence with
humility, without ever inventing anything.

## A flat country, between river and mountain.

Blainville is a city in the Lower Laurentians, on the north shore of Montreal, in the MRC (regional
county municipality) de Thérèse-De Blainville (Thérèse-De Blainville regional county municipality)
and the Metropolitan Community of Montreal. It is located around 45°40′ north latitude and 73°53′
west longitude. Area: approximately 55 km². Population: 59,819 inhabitants at the 2021 census;
61,114 in 2024, according to the current encyclopedic record. Kind: Blainvillois, Blainvilloise.
Mayor in 2026: Liza Poulin.

It’s not yet the mountain. This is the plain of the St. Lawrence Lowlands: marine clays, almost zero
slope, water that does not find its slope. The Rivière aux Chiens, a tributary of the Rivière des
Mille Îles, borders the south of the city. To the north and east, the wooded area continues towards
Sainte-Anne-des-Plaines. To the west: Boisbriand, Sainte-Thérèse, then Saint-Eustache, Lac des Deux
Montagnes, Oka and Kanehsatà:ke. To the south, on the other side of the river: Laval, Île Jésus.

Source: Environment page of the Ville de Blainville (City of Blainville). Woodlands and wetlands
partly overlap: a wooded peat bog can count in both.

The bog is not a point on a map. It is a complex: peat bogs, marshes, wooded and shrub swamps, wet
meadows, connected to streams which end in the river. The ministry describes a group of
approximately 622.7 hectares, included in the Atlas of territories of interest for conservation in
the St. Lawrence Lowlands.

#### Fauvel Lakes
Public woodland of 97 ha, inaugurated in 2017. Marshes, wooded peat bogs, two bodies of water, trail
of approximately 3.5 km. More than 100 species of birds announced by the City and its partners.

#### Bouchard map
Largely swampy woodland, footbridges, remains of a WWII munitions filling factory. The place where
industrial warfare bit into the damp country.

#### Trail to Sainte-Anne
A cycle path crosses the area and connects Blainville to Sainte-Anne-des-Plaines. It is often
through this that people discover the immensity of the peat bog – and, further on, the scars.

## Before names, there was a sea.

The bog was not “always there”. It is the last state of a hollow left by the ice, then by the sea,
then by a lake.

#### Champlain Sea
At the end of the last glaciation, the Laurentide ice sheet retreated. The ground, still buried
under the weight of the ice, allows the sea to enter. The Champlain Sea covers the Lowlands. The
syntheses of Occhietti and Richard place this episode around 13,000 to 10,500 years before today, in
calendar years, with variations depending on the sites and calibration. The entire Blainville plain
was then under salt water. Whales once swam where bungalows grow today. During this interval, no
human occupation is possible here: a seabed is not a camp.

#### Lake, then foam
The land rises, the sea recedes, and lakes remain trapped in marine clay, impermeable, sometimes
sensitive: the clay of the Champlain Sea. The City and the ministry write that the large peat bogs
in the Blainville woods could date back almost 6,000 years, and that the land targeted by Stablex is
surrounded by large peat bogs “which could date back 6,000 years”. The precise carbon-14 dating of
this specific complex remains to be documented by future university samples. This is an official
estimate, consistent with the age of many lowland peatlands, which begins in the mid-Holocene, once
the depressions were isolated.

#### How a bog is built
Sphagnum moss grows from the top and dies from the bottom. In cold, acidic, oxygen-poor water,
decomposition slows down. Peat piles up from a few tenths of a millimeter to a few millimeters per
year. In six millennia, this can create several meters of archive. An ombrotrophic peat bog ends up
receiving only rainwater. A fen, or fen, remains nourished by soil water, richer in minerals. The
Blainville complex combines the two, with marshes and swamps. Le Devoir, citing the documents in the
file, describes an ecosystem of approximately 6.2 km²: marshes, wooded and shrub swamps, wet meadows
and peat bogs.

The ministry writes that, in addition to habitat, hydrological regulation, sediment capture,
screening against wind and sun, and the landscape, “one of the important ecological functions to
preserve is that of a carbon sink”. He adds that the planned activities threaten the sustainability
of adjacent wetlands: hydrology, plant and microbial communities, water chemistry, soil quality. A
specialist cited by Le Devoir recalls that visible degradation is very difficult to reverse, and
that the disappearance of the complex could release the already sequestered carbon. The exact
quantitative assessment of the tonnage of carbon stored in this complex is part of the independent
environmental studies still to be investigated.

## Several nations, the same country of water.

Two errors respond to each other. Assign the bog to a single people. Or start the story in 1683,
with a lordship. A third would be to invent an oral story that no public source contains.

### What “unassigned” means here
For the island of Montreal and the south of the Laurentians, there is no numbered treaty by which a
nation ceded its title to the Crown, as in part of the West. The French lordships were granted
without the consent that contemporary law would recognize. Kanien’kehá:ka traditional authorities
maintain that their traditional territory was never ceded. The press, for the Kahnawà:ke nation,
describes it as extending from the St. Lawrence Valley to the Adirondacks and northern New York
State, including the Montreal region. McGill University, in its territorial recognition, names the
Kanien’kehá:ka guardians of the lands and waters of Tiohtià:ke. An educational document from UQTR
places the south of the Laurentians in the Kanien’kehá:ka ancestral territory, and the Weskarini, an
Anishinabe group, further north, in the Hautes-Laurentides.

No court judgment says: “the peat bog lot belongs to such and such a community”. The fact,
historical and political, is simpler. This country was not ceded by treaty. Several nations have
attachments to it that overlap in time. Making it an exclusive queue would be wrong.

#### Kanien’kehá:ka
Autonym: the people of flint. In Kanien'kéha, the traditional longhouse people are the
Kanonsonnkweonkwe (or Kannosonhkwe'ón:we; Rotinonhsón:ni), distinct from the Onondaga term
Haudenosaunee. Guardians of the Eastern Gate. Nearby communities: Kanehsatà:ke (Oka), Kahnawà:ke
(south of Montreal), Akwesasne. Tioweró:ton hunting territory, in the Laurentians, shared between
Kahnawà:ke and Kanehsatà:ke. The southern Laurentians are described as part of their ancestral
territory.

#### Anishinabeg, Nipissing, Abenaki
The Algonquins frequented Lac des Deux Montagnes and the Ottawa Valley. In 1674, Father Dalmas noted
an “Algonquin cabin” on Île Jésus. The Nipissing joined the Oka mission in 1727. The Abenakis named
the Mille Îles River Makigôtekw, the river of rakes.

#### St. Lawrence Iroquoians
Corn, squash and bean farmers, palisaded villages, including Hochelaga visited by Cartier in 1535.
Their sites in the valley disappeared between Cartier and Champlain (1603). Open debate: war,
epidemics, climate, adoption by the Wendat, the Haudenosaunee, the Algonquins, the Abenakis. They
did not “disappear without leaving anyone behind”; they ceased to exist as a distinct nation on the
river.

#### Wendat
Historical allies of the French, adversaries and then parents of the Haudenosaunee. Certain Wendat
traditions, and the work of archaeologists, link some of the dispersed St. Lawrence Iroquoians to
Wendat communities. Their direct presence on the Blainville peat bog is not documented. Their
history sheds light on the region. She doesn't own it alone.

A major political and legal distinction separates the Longhouse (traditional sovereign governance,
carried by the Clan Mothers, hereditary chiefs and the Great Law of Peace / Kaianerehkó:wa) from the
system of band councils imposed by the British Crown and the Indian Act to administer the colonial
regime. Bodies such as band councils or the Assembly of First Nations (AFN) are state creations
which in no way replace the inherent sovereignty of the peoples of the Long House
(Kanonsonnkweonkwe). To understand the political significance of this terminological and
institutional distinction, see the webinar on traditional sovereignty and Kannosonhkwe'ón:we ↗.

### An indigenous timeline of the country around
Paleoindian groups, hunters, entered southern Quebec as the ice retreated. The Blainville plain is
still marine, or has just emerged. No camp can be placed there without proof.

The Archaic intensifies in the valley: hunting, fishing, gathering, cut stone and polished stone.
This is also the order of magnitude used for the start of the peat bog. Human history and plant
history become contemporary, without a published archaeological site linking them to this precise
location.

Artifacts associated with the Mille Îles River, unearthed during excavations mentioned for 1996, are
estimated between 3000 BCE and the year 1000. The source is secondary: a general public synthesis
based on the archeology of the river. The interval covers the Late Archaic and the Woodland:
pottery, points, bank camps. The river is a road long before being a lordship.

In the valley, Iroquoian groups settled around corn. Hochelaga, on the island of Montreal, was a
stockaded village of around a thousand people when Cartier passed there in 1535. The exact site
remains debated. An open bog is not a village location: too wet, too acidic for corn. This is the
hinterland. Hunting, berries, plants, water, refuge.

The Iroquoian villages of the Saint Lawrence cease. Champlain, in 1603 and then in 1611, no longer
found Hochelaga. In European sources, the river once again becomes a corridor of passage, more than
a country of agricultural villages.

The Jesuit Relations describe Île Jésus, then Île Montmagny, “beautiful and large”, bordered to the
north by the Saint-Jean River – one of the ancient names of the Mille Îles River, associated with
Jean Nicolet.

Father Antoine Dalmas tours Jesus Island. He finds two indigenous camps. One is noted as an
“Algonquin cabin”. The island serves as a stopover, especially in the summer, for the fur trade.
Artifacts from these passages are still reported.

The colonization of Jesus Island stalled, partly because of Iroquois opposition. On September 24,
1683, according to the municipal timeline, Michel-Sidrac Dugué de Boisbriand received the lordship
of Mille-Îles, from Terrebonne to the Rivière du Chêne, in Saint-Eustache. The Canadian Encyclopedia
indicates 1673. Both dates are in circulation. The City retains 1683. A concession is not proof of
an empty country.

The Sault-au-Récollet mission is moved to Lac des Deux Montagnes. In 1721, villages opened:
Kanien'kehá:ka on one side, Algonquins and soon Nipissing on the other, under the Sulpicians. The
king grants a lordship on the condition, according to several readings, that the lands are used by
the Aboriginal people. The Sulpicians will then claim ownership. The conflict is not peripheral. It
is the origin of Kanehsatà:ke, about thirty kilometers west of the peat bog. It will break out in
1990.

Algonquin converts from the mission often spent the winter on their hunting grounds in the Ottawa
Valley, and the summer near the lake. The Kanien’kehá:ka of the mission remain more attached to the
village. The south of the Laurentians is a country crossed, hunted, claimed. It is not a blank page
between two clearings.

Patriots from Saint-Eustache, led in particular by Amury Girod, marched towards Oka and Kanehsatà:ke
to look for weapons and “disarm the savages”, according to judicial sources of the time. The same
fall, a patriot expedition to Kahnawà:ke failed: the Kanien’kehá:ka captured the attackers and
delivered them to Montreal. The school history of the Patriots often omits this chapter. However, it
takes place on the same river.

Battle of Saint-Eustache. Colborne attempts to cross the Mille Îles River. Nearly 70 Patriots were
killed. The village is pillaged. The river is a battle line, not a setting.

Creation of the Doncaster reserve, Tioweró:ton, hunting and fishing territory in the Laurentians,
for the use of the Mohawks of Kahnawà:ke and Kanesatake. It's not the bog. This is proof that the
relationship with the Laurentian Forest did not end with the seigneurial regime.

Oka crisis, or Kanehsatà:ke resistance. A golf course and a residential project threaten a pine
forest which includes a cemetery. Seventy-eight days. A police officer killed. The army. The Mercier
Bridge blocked by Kahnawà:ke. The conflict comes from a title that has never been settled since
1717. Blainville is not Oka. It’s the same basin, and the same story: wet and wooded lands treated
as empty, because a European land register saw neither the deaths nor the uses.

## Understanding colonial archives and honoring indigenous memory.

Conventional municipal stories traditionally begin with the seigneurial concession of 1683,
Marie-Thérèse de Blainville or the Bouchard Plan, retracing an urban history of more than 300 years.
However, the plant landscape and the memory of the territory extend over several millennia.

Three essential factors enable us to understand the nature of written archives:

1. A lifestyle that respects the environment. Wetlands and peatlands were used for hunting, seasonal
gathering (small fruits, medicinal plants) and land or river routes. This respectful relationship
with the land left a light mark on the landscape, without the construction of permanent sedentary
structures in the swamp.

2. The prism of colonial archives. European chroniclers and missionaries mainly recorded the
establishment of parishes, censives and trading posts, often perceiving wetlands as obstacles rather
than nurturing ecosystems.

3. Future archaeological projects. The banks of the Rivière aux Chiens and the surroundings of the
Mille Îles basin constitute areas of potential archaeological interest whose in-depth study must be
considered in close consultation with the nations holding these lands.

The margins of the peat bog — drier cords, terraces of the Rivière aux Chiens, approaches to the
Rivière des Mille Îles — represent major ecological transition zones. The heart of the peat complex
constitutes a valuable paleoenvironmental archive: ancient pollens, sediments and traces of past
ecosystems. The detailed analysis of pollen cores in Blainville represents an open area of
​​scientific research to better understand the plant and climatic history of the Lower Laurentians.

## Makigôtekw, the road that holds the whole country together.

The Mille Îles River is an outlet from Lac des Deux Montagnes, therefore from the Outaouais, to the
Rivière des Prairies. Around 42 kilometers, a drop of around fifteen meters, around a hundred
islands covering around 350 hectares. It forms the northern limit of Île Jésus. Without it, the peat
bog is just a basin. With it, the Blainville wetland complex belongs to a living corridor, from Oka
to Terrebonne.

In European sources, it was called the Saint-Jean River from the Relation of 1637, then the Jesus
River, the La Chesnaye River, the Repentigny River, and Branch of the River Ottawa. The French name
comes from “Mil Isles”, the archipelago between the lake and Terrebonne, fixed as the name of a
watercourse in the 18th century. Boucault wrote “Milse Isle” in 1754. The Abenaki name, Makigôtekw,
rakes river, is older than the seigneury. The "rakes" have been read as fisheries, or as the comb
shape of channels between islands. Both readings remain glosses.

#### Wildlife refuge, 1998
Ten islands, 26.2 ha, in Laval and Rosemère, managed with Éco-Nature. Regulation which protects in
particular the habitat of the map turtle, the brown snake, the wood duck and the Canada goose.

#### 92 status species
The refuge and its partners target, among others, the copper redhorse, the river redhorse, the
eastern sand darter, the channel darter, the grass shiner, the American water-willow, the least
bittern, the Canada warbler, turtles, bats, and the monarch.

#### A fish that only exists here
The copper redhorse, Moxostoma hubbsi, is endemic to southwestern Quebec, endangered. Its critical
habitat includes portions of the Mille Îles and Des Prairies rivers. He does not live in the bog. It
lives in the river that drains it. Cutting one means impoverishing the other.

In 2016, La Revue de Terrebonne recalled an often cited figure: 99.9% of the Canadian population of
American carmantine, a bank plant, is found along this watercourse. This is a widely publicized
conservation fact. Before legal use, it remains to be re-verified in the official CDPNQ records.

Éco-Nature suggests that the Mille Îles River is home to more animal and plant species than
Mont-Tremblant National Park. Mediation formula, not a statistical table reproduced here. The
intuition holds: the wealth is in the islands, the seagrass beds and the riverside marshes, not in
the altitude.

## The soft soil protected the bog. A time.

The seigneury of Mille-Îles encompasses what will become Saint-Eustache, Bois-des-Filion, Lorraine,
Rosemère, Boisbriand, Sainte-Thérèse and Blainville. The City writes that it was necessary to wait
until the second half of the 18th century for the territory of Blainville to open up, very
gradually, under the leadership of Marie-Thérèse de Blainville, daughter of Louis-Jean-Baptiste
Céloron de Blainville, and her husband Jean-Marie Nolan Lamarque. The first inhabitant often cited,
along the river, is Joseph Phillion, whose name moved towards Bois-des-Filion.

Why so late, when Montreal was founded in 1642? Because it’s not easy wheat growing land. Forest,
clay, stagnant water, distance and, in the 17th century, military insecurity. Peatlands are the last
plots that agriculture desires. You don't plow a carpet of sphagnum moss. We bypass it, we drain it
if we can, we despise it.

Disagreement between Lord Hertel and Lordship Lamarque. The seigneury is split by the Grande Ligne,
today Boulevard du Curé-Labelle, route 117. The Canadian Encyclopedia dates the episode to 1791 and
speaks of a mill conflict. The north-south axis of the city comes from this dispute, not from an old
published path.

Sainte-Thérèse became a municipality in 1849. The surrounding area was established as the parish of
Sainte-Thérèse-de-Blainville. The modern municipal regime is established on a seigneurial land
register.

Detachments: Boisbriand, Rosemère, Bois-des-Filion. Blainville is what remains, then what refuses to
merge with Sainte-Thérèse.

City status, under the Boisvert administration. The municipal page remembers June 29. Secondary
notices say June 14. Eight mayors since: Roger Boisvert, André De Carufel, Paul Mercier, Oneil
Charron, Pierre Gingras, François Cantin, Richard Perreault, Liza Poulin.

The name of the city does not come from an indigenous toponym. It comes from an officer,
Jean-Baptiste Céloron de Blainville. Renaming does not erase the previous country. This only makes
it unreadable on signs.

## What we do with land deemed worthless.

The big turning point was not 1968. It was 1941. The federal government expropriated, in a few
weeks, a huge piece of land in the parish to set up Plan Bouchard, a munitions filling complex. It
is not an explosives factory in the strict sense, but an assembly workshop: tracer bullets, shells,
bombs, mines, depth charges, torpedoes. Local sources speak of approximately 6,000 acres, some 339
buildings, and 6,000 to 8,000 workers. The Canadian Encyclopedia records 1939 and 6,000 employees.
The numbers vary. The scale, no. A temporary industrial town grows in the damp woodland.

The war ended in 1945. The complex became Camp Bouchard, a depot and training camp, until around
1969 according to the encyclopedia. In 1981, the City acquired other federal acres and sold part of
them to Stablex. Local heritage notices speak of 12 acres. The military site was decontaminated
around 1995. This does not resolve the chemical story that begins next door.

#### Stablex, since 1983
A subsidiary linked to an American group, Stablex processes inorganic residual hazardous materials
by stabilization and solidification, the Sealosafe process. The residues are mixed with cementitious
binders, then buried. Five historic cells. Cell 6, planned on neighboring municipal land, would
target more than eight million tonnes over around forty years, a horizon sometimes announced until
2067.

#### The bog as trash
Part of the complex was used as a dump. In October 2014, a citizen and municipal cleanup removed
more than 61,000 liters of waste and seven hunting caches. Nest boxes are placed for the tree
swallow, the northern flicker and the downy woodpecker. The tires and the ocher pools along the
track have unwillingly become the landscape through which many discover the place.

There is a continuity, moral as well as administrative, between the seigneurial judgment (“useless
for the plow”), the military judgment (“perfect for explosives, no one lives there”) and the
industrial judgment (“site already disturbed, low value”). The BAPE and the studies commissioned by
the company itself have cracked this latest judgment. They did not prevent it from becoming law.

## The species, with their degree of proof.

Each sheet carries its degree of proof. A magnificent plant cited in a petition does not have the
weight of a fish counted in a report submitted to the ministry. Both deserve to be named. Not in the
same voice.

### Threatened and vulnerable species — Grande Tourbière de Blainville
Collaborative citizen science inventory documenting the presence, distribution and status of species
observed in the peatland. New species recorded by naturalists and citizens are synchronized live
below.

#### Latest observations verified in the field:

### Fathead
49 individuals in the inventory corrected for captures in the Stablex file. Small fish of ponds and
slow-moving streams. Its abundance says: calm, warm water, not a trout river.

### Shadow of slime
4 captures. It is the emblematic fish of oxygen-poor peat bogs: it comes up to swallow the air.
Finding it here is more telling than a fishing trophy. The corrected report specifies that no brook
trout were caught.

### Five-spine stickleback
1 capture in the same patch. Kind of small vegetated environments. Le Devoir, citing company
documents, also speaks of nine species of fish in the sector: the complete list of these nine names
was not in the public extracts read for this file.

### Copper Knight
Endangered, endemic. Critical habitat in sections of the Mille Îles River, not in the acidic pools
of the peat bog. The link is hydrological: what leaves the complex ends in its river.

### Rock digger, sand darter, grass minnow
Fish with status of the river refuge. They talk about the seagrass beds and the sandy bottoms of the
corridor, not the carpet of sphagnum moss.

### Sphagnum moss
The City and naturalist descriptions agree: the ancient lake was invaded by sphagnum moss. It’s not
a species, it’s the profession of the place. Acidifies, fills with water, archives pollen, stores
carbon.

### Virginia Woodwardie
Rare fern from the peat bogs of southern Quebec, likely to be designated. Ecological studies confirm
a highly suitable habitat on the site, and field naturalists have reported observations to be
documented during botanical monitoring.

### Follicle sedge and Virginia St. John's wort
Targeted by the ministry in questions to Stablex, with precise inventory windows: sedge from
mid-July to early October, St. John's wort from August to September, woodwardie from June to
October. The fact that we require these passages speaks to the quality of the habitat, not yet the
result.

### Pitcher plant
Carnivorous plant emblematic of open peat bogs. The urns fill with rainwater and digest insects: an
elegant response to soil without nitrogen. Much photographed by the defenders of the site. Not yet
seen in a public ministerial table consulted here. Ecologically, its presence would be normal.

### Rossolis
Two sundews, glue traps on round or spatulate leaves. Same logic as the pitcher plant: the nitrogen
comes from midges, not from the soil. A single insect feeds the plant for weeks. Repeated citizen
reports.

### Labrador tea
Aromatic bog shrub. Leaves infused in much of the indigenous Northeast. If a single plant were to
link the botanical record and the indigenous record, it would be this one — provided we do not
invent an uncollected local ceremony for it.

### Cottongrass, kalmia, cassander, larch
The classic costume of a southern Quebec peat bog: cotton grass, kalmias, caliculated cassander,
andromeda, cranberry, tamarack and sometimes black spruce on the mounds, red maple in the swamps. To
be confirmed species by species. Do not check them off like an inventory.

### American carmantine
Threatened riverbank plant, most of whose Canadian population is linked to the Mille Îles River.
Immediate neighborhood, not the boggy heart.

### Four-toed salamander
Small, white belly with black stars, four toes on the hind legs. It lays eggs in sphagnum moss.
Radio-Canada cited it in 2016 among the species with status in the Fauvel lakes corridor. A youth
media shows her in 2026 as present in the Grande Tourbière (Great Peat Bog). Precarious status in
Quebec. It is probably the signature animal of the place, pending a published inventory.

### painted turtle
Le Devoir writes that the land intended to be razed is a habitat for the painted turtle, listed
under Canada's Species at Risk Act. Supporting photo in the article of March 19, 2025. Presence
among the most solid in the herpetological file.

### Snapping turtle
Large muddy bottom turtle, of concern in Canada. Citizen list of the sector, and species monitored
by the river refuge. Credible. Not a capture cited in the ministerial extracts read.

### Map turtle
River turtle, map on the shell, protected habitat in the refuge. Citizen lists also put it near the
peat bog. Its true home is the current and the islands, where it winters at the bottom, protected
from frost.

### wood turtle
Threatened. Le Devoir cites it among the theoretically protected animals which risk being killed by
the works, with a “like” which may only be an example of the category. It is not counted here as
confirmed on the lot, as long as an inventory mention is not public.

### Milk and green snakes
Two status snakes, cited by the citizen appeals of April 2025. The spotted snake is also a target of
the riverside refuge. The brown snake is named in the refuge regulations, not in the peat bog lists.

### The sky of the complex
Nearly 200 species of birds recorded in the project area, according to Le Devoir. On the site
itself, a ministerial report mentions 132 species, including 66% nesting and 34% probable or
possible nesting. Families mentioned: warblers, ducks, woodpeckers, birds of prey, shorebirds,
buntings, thrushes, herons. The Fauvel lakes alone are reported to have more than 100 species.

### Common Nightjar
Threat. Named by Le Devoir among the endangered species recorded in the area. Niche on the ground,
in openings. Spring deforestation falls exactly on schedule.

### Canada Warbler and Eastern Pewee
Two undergrowth birds in decline, cited in the same article. The Canada warbler is also followed by
the river refuge. Their presence says that the complex is not just a marsh: there is mature humid
forest, or fairly continuous for these species.

### Common Yellowthroat, Blue-headed Vireo
The common yellowthroat is considered a potential nester in the area, in decline due to loss of
habitat. The blue-headed vireo was photographed on the coveted ground. Neither are ornithologist’s
curiosities: they are birds of forest continuity.

### Bobolink
Threatened, bird of wet meadows. Noted at Fauvel lakes by amateur ornithologists, and shown in 2026
by youth media as a Grande Tourbière species. The wastelands and meadows of the complex suit it
better than the rounded bog.

### Least bittern
The smallest heron, threatened, dependent on cattail marshes. Target of the riverside refuge and
citizen lists in the area. Discreet to the point that you miss it if you don't listen at dawn.

### Great blue heron, chimney swift
The great blue heron is commonplace and magnificent on the river; citizens also put it near ponds.
The endangered chimney swift was cited in 2016 for the Fauvel corridor. It nests in chimneys and
large hollow trees, not in sphagnum moss.

### What we see at Fauvel lakes
Local notes, linked to eBird, mention kingfisher, kingfisher, tawny thrush, cuckoo, bobolink,
melodious vireo, buntings, warblers, ducks, waders. This is a sample of a public park, not the
secret of the inaccessible heart of the bog.

### Moose
Le Devoir confirms the moose in the ecosystem of approximately 6.2 km². A moose in a suburban bog,
half an hour from downtown, is already a political fact. He needs continuity. A landfill cell in the
middle of the complex is a fracture, not the loss of “a few trees.”

### Deer, beaver, muskrat, fox
Cited by petitions and naturalists. All ecologically expected. The beaver is also an engineer: its
dams can maintain exactly the water level on which the peat bog depends. Killing it or drying up its
pond is rewriting hydrology.

### Bats
Little brown bat, northern myotis, eastern pipistrelle: species at risk monitored in the wildlife
corridor of the Mille Îles River. Swamps and mature woodlands constitute prime hunting grounds for
these nocturnal insectivores.

### Monarch and rusty-spotted bumblebee
Two status insects tracked on the Mille Îles River. The monarch needs milkweed, rather in an open
environment than in an acidic bog. The rusty-patched bumblebee is on the brink of continental
extinction. Mentioning them reminds us that the corridor does not stop at vertebrates.

### What the number “742 species” cannot carry
A citizen mobilization in April 2025 claims that 742 species have been listed in the cell 6 sector.
The figure is circulating. There is no official total in the ministerial documents cited by the
press. The solid public totals are other: nearly 200 birds in the sector, 132 on the site in a
report, nine fish mentioned in the sector, three species named in the catch correction, 97 hectares
and more than 100 birds at Fauvel lakes, 92 species with status in the riverine refuge. A broad
citizen inventory can be true without being quotable as a state figure. To reject it out of
contempt, or to engrave it as truth, are two lazinesses.

### Why is this living being “from the south” and “from the north” at the same time?
The peat bogs of the alluvial plain, like those of Lanoraie further east on the north shore, are
refuges: they keep northern plants (dwarf birch, low willows) and southern plants (Virginia
woodwardie) far from the heart of their area. Blainville is in the same natural province. This is
why a suburban peat bog can contain flora that you would think were boreal, next to a highway. The
comparison with the Tourbières-de-Lanoraie ecological reserve is the best mirror available: same
family of environments, real protection there, industrial file here.

## The country does not stop at the city sign.

#### Sainte-Anne-des-Plaines and Mirabel
The track and wetlands continue towards the north-east, in a long agricultural plain. Mirabel has
lost immense wetlands to the benefit of the airport and crops. The Blainville complex is one of the
last large connected pieces. Jérôme Dupras, ecological economist, reminded us: in a region which has
already lost the majority of its natural environments, this sector is one of the rare areas of
connectivity that remain.

#### Boisbriand, Rosemère, Lorraine, Sainte-Thérèse
Same lordship, same clay, same parish detachments from the 20th century. Rosemère touches the refuge
of the islands. The Rosemère woodland and the footbridges to Darling Island are part of the same
waterfront network. To think “Blainville only” is to cut living things in two.

#### Laval, Jesus Island
Algonquin stops attested in 1674. Bank archaeology. Today, Rivière-des-Mille-Îles Park is the most
successful public gateway to understanding the corridor. Exploration center, kayaking, refuge
islands. It is there, more than in a dump parking lot, that we see the redhorse, the map turtle, the
great blue heron.

#### Saint-Eustache and Deux-Montagnes
1837. The river as a front. The church burned, the village pillaged. A few kilometers away,
Kanehsatà:ke, already a century-old mission, already a contested title. School history separates
“our” Patriots and “the” Natives. The terrain puts them on the same river, the same week.

#### Oka, Kanehsatà:ke, national park
Mission of 1721. Pine forest. Graveyard. Golf. 1990. Today, the Kanehsatà:ke powwow, one of the
oldest in Quebec, and still young indigenous tourism in the Lower Laurentians. The Oka National Park
occupies part of the landscape that the Sulpicians opened to resorts. To visit the park without
knowing who owns the pine forest next door is to miss half the place.

#### Kahnawà:ke and Akwesasne
Not adjoining the bog, but parts of the same nation. Kahnawà:ke was born from a Jesuit seigneury of
Sault-Saint-Louis, granted in 1680, since reduced. Current demands refuse to be confined within the
seigniorial perimeter. When a Blainville petition says “this territory is unceded”, it takes up this
position. It does not, in itself, create legal title to the municipal lot.

#### Terrebonne, Isle of Moulins
Where the Mille Îles River joins the Prairies. Bird sanctuary, seagrass beds, seasonal fishing ban
to protect spawning, permanent ban on the copper redhorse. The blue route, a navigable route,
reminds us that the Outaouais, the lake and the Mille Îles are a single route.

#### Lanoraie, the protected mirror
Tourbières-de-Lanoraie ecological reserve, in the alluvial plain, further east. Virginia woodwardie,
dwarf birch, moose, beaver, some 140 species of birds, and a bog butterfly, the Lanoraie elfin.
Proof that the State knows how to protect this type of environment when it decides to do so. Proof,
too, of what a complete inventory can reveal once it is financed.

## 2023–2026: the peat bog enters history through injury.

Stablex submits the rearrangement of cell 6. The BAPE holds a hearing. Report 371, submitted on
September 8, 2023, notes that the land is part of a corridor connecting two wetland complexes deemed
exceptional, and that the project would fragment it. The commission does not recommend
authorization. In September, the City adopted an ecological transition plan which calls for the
protection of peatlands.

Expert opinion from the ministry, June 2024, published only on April 4, 2025: the adjacent wetlands
have a high ecological value, a large part of which is included in the Lowlands Atlas. The ministry
writes in black and white the risk to the sustainability of the complex.

Despite the BAPE, Quebec tabled a special law, referred to in the press as the Loi 93 (Bill 93), and
forced the City to cede the land. Minister Maïté Blanchette-Vézina states on several occasions that
this is an industrial site without a peat bog “for a long time”. The Englobe study, obtained by Le
Devoir, describes 278,046 m² of wetlands on the ground, including 97,237 m² of peatlands,
approximately double the wetland area destroyed in the Northvolt file, a comparison made by the
newspaper. The table of ecological functions from the same study gives a high yield to the site's
peatlands for biodiversity, climate, water regulation and natural screen. However, Stablex speaks of
“low ecological value”.

Blainville and the CMM contest. An alternative proposal would have added 123 hectares of protected
wetlands on municipal land, to consolidate the Grande Tourbière. The Court rejects the request for
an injunction. The call follows. The destruction work begins. The decree still requires an update of
the wildlife inventory: at the time of authorization, the State did not have all the data. Fisheries
and Oceans Canada indicated in March that federal authorization for fish habitat had not been
requested. The ministry then requires at least fifteen years of monitoring of the impacts on
adjacent environments.

The “Save the Grande Tourbière” petition has around 47,000 signatures at the beginning of 2026.
Dozens of municipalities and the UMQ support Blainville. Scientists sign letters. On April 8, 2025,
Le Devoir headlines on the peat bog several thousand years old. In 2026, William Guindon, then a
teenager, filed a complaint with the Office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Human
Rights, on behalf of future generations, after having exhausted, he says, the remedies here. La
Presse meets him on July 3, 2026 in the Fauvel lakes woodland. The Aces of Info present him at 14
years old in July 2026. The official site then says he is 15 years old.

Le Devoir reports that water and sediment samples, taken by Climat Québec and the Coalition of
Blainville Citizens near the plant, contain several toxic metals. Citizen campaign relayed by a
major daily newspaper, not a conclusion from the ministry. It is enough to say that the chemical
question is not theoretical, and that it goes beyond deforestation alone.

The government's position, to be fair, can be summed up in three sentences. Quebec would need a
capacity to bury stabilized hazardous materials, with cell 5 approaching its end. The land belonging
to the City would, according to the minister, already be industrial. The special law would avoid a
“break in service”. The position of the studies, including those of the ministry and the company's
agent, is based on three others. There is peat bog on and around the lot. It is exceptional on the
scale of the Lowlands. Destroying it or drying it out is not reversible on the scale of a human
life, or even several.

## Knowledge and data to deepen together.

Radiocarbon dating of the base of the peat bog, exhaustive pollen diagrams, precise assessment of
the carbon stock and the complete floristic inventory are among the essential independent scientific
projects to be pursued to protect this environment.

The ancestral toponymy and living history of these lands are part of the oral tradition of the
families of Kanehsatà:ke, Kahnawà:ke and surrounding nations. This traditional knowledge is not
measured by the yardstick of colonial administrative archives: it is listened to from communities,
with full respect for their sovereignty.
